I remember last semester, during spring break, while I was figuring out the code for the e-ink display and making the poetry generator, I was talking to Phil and Nikolai, asking them how I should make it work without a battery. I wanted to make the microcontroller run on supercapacitors, like Allison's poetry generator. The battery changes the concept of the project slightly. I wanted it to be a more direct solar energy mechanism. Phil told me about a mechanism for that to work, but figuring out that circuit would have been hard in that limited time. However, I learnt recently how to run a microcontroller with supercapacitors. But while I was talking to them, I was also chatting away about my initial '100 days of making' idea, which was making the beam bots every day, so I could get better at circuits. I was telling them how I want to start making physical things and have that as a medium for storytelling. Phil mentioned that most of us on the floor do want to make physical things.

But I started wanting to make physical things long before that. I think I started questioning while eating pizza with Gabe and arjun. arjun asked us whether we'd apply for residency, and he said something along the lines, "You'd apply for hypercinema, right?” Again, I don't remember it verbatim, but at that point, I realised that I wouldn't, though, because I didn't enjoy hypercinema in the first semester, nor the technology that was taught in that course, as much as I would have thought. I enjoyed it when we worked with Stop Motion Studio, but Unity and everything else, not particularly. I've seen people work with Motion Capture, and I have realised how I wouldn't want to work with that. Maybe it was also the incorporation of AI in moving image that swayed me away from it. I don't know why. Most of the moving image tools don't seem appealing to me at all. Nor am I mesmerized by it. Although I like Alanna's thesis quite a bit. I feel like that's quite different than the tools that we learn in hypercinema. It's more code that is taught in computational narratives. There was this simplicity to her project that I really liked.

I do enjoy simpler technology, I think. I don't enjoy complicated technology, maybe, because it also takes a while to pick up, but sometimes I don't understand the point. I like cameras. Taking photos and videos with them. But I like ‘my’ camera. I kind of picked it up myself, amateurishly. I don't know how to use another camera thoroughly. So I can't just pick up any DSLR. I can use my phone for photos and videos. I can simply use my iPad to animate. I enjoy drawing and writing. 

Now, fabrication. I am not sure. I've barely fabricated. But I've noticed that I enjoy sculpting with wires a lot. Ruth Asawa's work stuck with me. I enjoyed making the flower for ‘Energy’, and I like making rings with Kezia.

I do like coding a lot. But the fun of coding is in coming up with algorithms and spending hours debugging. I like circuits, also for the same reasons. It teases my brain in a way that I like — the problem-solving.

I sometimes love reading code too. I like looking at the JSON file of the ‘Rewiring connections’ project more than the drawings themselves. The JSON file tells a story. I like reading arjun's code and his comments. There's a lot of thought behind the variable names and the structure. It's similar to the manner in which Allison also writes code in the class. That's why I like working in Python, because it's close to English. Because they put a lot of care in choice. The thoroughness is why I like arjun's exposed circuits too. 

I was talking to Matt sometime in June. We were walking to the train after Prisha's birthday. I was telling him how I like to closely observe people, which was when he was talking about how he closely observes materials. I don't pay as much close attention to materials as such. I should, though. I am observant. I have to start employing that a bit more for objects and things around me, I think.

Even while talking to Megan about form versus subject, I've realised, like most people, I often get mesmerized by subject. Maybe because I don't observe form as well. Well, in some cases, I do enjoy form. (Not to beat a dead horse) Like in Ruth Asawa's work. The structure and how it's made are visible. 

Differing from Ruth Asawa's work. I can't remember whose work it was that Cody was showing to me, but he did talk about autobiographical work having this exposed aspect, which is different from the structural exposure. I think the exposed aspect there shows vulnerability. I like Cody's 'Kinetic Randomness Generator'. The form also plays a big role there — the shape of the bodies. The form is telling of the project. (I suppose I still need to read more about form and subject. This is what I'm saying based on my current understanding.)

I also sometimes question: what is the need to make something interactive? What is the purpose? What does interaction add to a project? The kinetic projects in ‘Energy’ do actually need interaction. Sometimes, projects can be made without interaction, I think. Matt's spring show project, 'Inside Our Computers', the interaction is the core of the project, because that's how electricity is generated. Rios's 'Wearable Synthesizer' too. It charges up in that manner. Well, in both of these cases, the interaction itself generates the power for the project to work. There is a structural need — the interaction completes the structures here.

So why do I want to make physical things? There is a lot of influence from my cohort, definitely, and the works that I admire here. So, a little herd mentality is present. But it’s clear that I am definitely drawn to it, as well.

My medium, though, with more clarity, is drawing and writing. It's vulnerable too. But I like wires, code, circuits, as a form of structure. I would want my projects to incorporate that structural need, that the interaction completes the structure aspect. I don't like them being hidden. I want to show the complexity through those.

Maybe there's a link here with structural and personal exposure that I like. It does tell me that I like exposure. But this still isn't solving why I want to make physical things.

I do think there's something there with being able to observe the more invisible infrastructures. It shows complexity, I think.

All this thinking, only to show that I like exposed work, that the interaction is part of the structure in some ways. That’s a good place to reach.

Maybe I just want to make physical things, because it deems direct exposure.

And, I guess, I should focus on the structural needs of the project, if I'm interested in that, rather than whether I want to make it physical or not.
