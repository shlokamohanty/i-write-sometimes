All of these might be explained in terms of strings, which may in turn be reduced to a more fundamental entity, 'we don't know what'.

Could we be in two states at once and not know it, just like the physicist in his container? Or if we are in a definite state, whose observation is responsible for this?

But if consciousness is required to turn ghostly probability waves into things that are more or less like the objects we meet in everyday life, we will not be able to say that matter is what would be there anyway, whether or not human minds were around (at least if we assume that human minds are all the minds there are).

Penrose speculates that this 'objective reduction' takes place in specific parts of the neurons that make up the brain (so-called microtubules).

The conscious mind would then be the result, rather than the cause, of these quantum-processes.

For this reason, the explanations of biological processes can be said to be reducible to chemical and ultimately to physical ones.

We now have a way of expressing space-time points as sets of four positive or negative numbers, x, y, z, and, t, where x, y, and z, represent the three spatial dimensions and t the temporal dimension. Mathematicians have found a way of reducing numbers to something yet more fundamental: sets.

According to the Platonic understanding, mathematical objects exist in a 'third realm', a world distinct from the world of matter, on the one hand, and the world of mental entities, such as perceptions, thoughts, and feelings, on the other.

Understanding of mathematical as fundamentally mental in nature. They are of the same kind as the other things that pass through our mind: thoughts and plans and concepts and ideas. Yet they are not wholly subjective.

For the scientific reductionist sets out to reduce the human mind to the activity of the human brain, then reduce the brain to an assembly of interacting cells, the cells to molecules, the molecules to atoms, the atoms to subatomic particles, the subatomic particles to collection of space-time points, the collections of space-time points to sets of numbers, and the sets of numbers to pure sets. But at the very end of this reduction, we now seem to loop right back to where we came from: to mental entities.

Are persons real? - Chapter 3

At the fundamental level, there are not just pieces of matter, but also systems that provide a perspective on the pieces of matter, systems with a point of view.

That we move through time as more or less the same person, and that we are the central control room where data from the world get in, and from where decisions to act are sent out.

Our self, it appears, is whatever inhabits the body that sustains the functions of the mind.

René Descartes stressed that such unification was necessary for a self (or soul) to gain knowledge of the world

This picture of the mind is sometimes described as the 'Cartesian theatre'.

When you read the sentence 'The man ran out of the house, before that he had kissed his wife', the sequence in which the information comes in is 'running - kissing', but the sequence of events you construct from this is 'kissing - running'

If each of them is a continuation of you, you cannot have unified self, since the six selves will develop in very different directions, have different experiences, and make different choices, while a single self is supposed to develop only in one direction, have one set of experiences, and make one kind of choices.

Memetics, how the development of the life of the mind can be understood by the replication of non-material idea-like replicators, the memes. A meme can be as basic as the idea of boiling food before eating it, or of transporting loads by means of wheels, or it can constitute a highly complex structure of ideas like the game of Go, Euclidean geometry, Pānini's Sanskrit grammar, perspectival drawing, and, of course, memetics itself.


Is time real? - Chapter 4

Gödel pointed out that an objective flow of time, time understood according to the A-series, has to imply that reality, understood as everything there is, grows in time.

Many thinkers, Gödel amongst them, have therefore referred to save the notion of absolute reality by rejecting the idea of an objective flow of time, of a reality that grows as a merely potential future moves into an actual present.

The kind of time the existence of which could be guaranteed by these observations would have nothing to do with the time as it appears to us: whether or not it exists, the world would look just the same. It would not even be clear why we would want to call it time at all, rather than, say, 'the way matter and motion are distributed on a cosmic scale'.

Times comes in three flavours, past, present, and future. If we ask ourselves whether time is real, we have to specify which (if any) of these three parts of time is real.

If the object behaves like a particle, it will go through one of the two slits; if it behaves like a wave, it will show the familiar interference pattern on the other side of the barrier.

There is no shared, objective, observer-independent present reality, but only a real-for-me, real-for-you, and so on.

While the present is tremendously important for the way the world appears to us (because it is when the world appears to us), it does not have any claim to reality according to apocalyptic understanding of the term.

In the Newtonian world, time is the great unifier that coordinates events everywhere in the universe.
