I don't remember the first time I ever tasted that citrus fruit in my mouth. But I remember the first time that I tasted an orange again, after two or maybe three years. One thing you wouldn't know is the million thoughts that go through my head, the idea of something going down my throat. So the orange, the citrus, the sour, the sweet, I bit into it, knowing that maybe this might be my last time. Maybe after I eat this orange, my throat will close up. The tongue would enlarge so much that it would cover the entire mouth. I won't be able to breathe. That's it, anaphylaxis. I wonder if my body would be in a graveyard. Wouldn't it be funny if my tombstone had 'died from anaphylaxis' on it? Or would they burn my body? Who is going to tell them that I'm an atheist and that I would want to donate my body to 'science'? Can you tell them to dissect my brain thoroughly, so both you and I can understand? But will anyone even care? This is the one time my body will decide that the Allegra that I used to take won't work when I eat this orange. But I do it anyway. 

Did my throat close up? No. I could feel it go down my throat, the pulp touching the food pipe, everything. I kept swallowing to make sure, yes, my throat hasn't closed. Spoke a little extra to make sure my voice isn't going hoarse. Touched my face to make sure nothing was erupting on it. I wish I didn't think about death every time I ate. I'd choke on my words to tell you this, but maybe I'd actually choke before. Thus, I'd peel oranges for you, and I, till I die, or maybe not. So I eat oranges now.

---

I grew up with hypochondriacs. A small thing, exaggerated into a million possible conclusions. My grandpa is on benzos, and it's a casual affair. A heartbeat slightly uneven, and a pill, a solution widely available. There was a medicine cabinet. Why go to a pharmacist? You name it — prescription or otherwise — it's all there. One thing about growing up in India is that it's so easily accessible for the rich. Why is it that during COVID, there's a black market for oxygen cylinders on Twitter? That money makes it all so easily available, so easily accessible, a privilege I had. Money can buy a gold-package blood test, for you to second-guess your HbA1c levels, even though they are actually in the normal range. Or a glucose monitor to keep tracking your sugar level, even though sugar is completely fine, a small spike raises an alarm.

But, anyway, this is my story. I've been on benzos, amitriptyline, sumatriptans, some really high dose of NSAIDs, and a bunch of other pain medicines and asthma medicines and inhalers — those that make your potassium drop so much that your hands shake. I've had drinks on those medicines, supervised, of course. Yes, you read it, 'supervised'. Remember this is a story of me growing up, 16, 17, 18. But even before that, I grew up a sick child. CT scans, hospital lights. I'm really claustrophobic, though, so when they put me in the MRI machine, I created a ruckus. The tight space, the sound of the machine, all I could hear was that. I couldn't take it. Perhaps I still cannot. But, if I had a brain tumor, my CT scan would show anyway, right?

---

I grew up a sick child. 

I couldn't tell you that I'm actually terrified of eating sweet potatoes. I just ate it. Because I'd rather die than be embarrassed, right? It's more than just embarrassment, though. It's too much for anyone. This sweet potato would kill me because it once showed up in a blood allergy report, but I'd eaten sweet potatoes before, and nothing happened. So I ate it again and drank lots of water. You weren't able to tell I'm anxious over a sweet potato — that's silly. Maybe then I'd have died in your arms, of someone I love? But someone I couldn't tell I'm scared of sweet potatoes, because it's never fun to be with someone anxiety-ridden. Please don't make me open up. Make me vulnerable, and my anxiety will come pouring out, and you'll leave. It did, and I couldn't tell you. And you left, you had to. How could you? I had so much love for you. I had so much love for you. It suffocated you, didn't it? So, you stopped talking to me. 

But I couldn't tell you that I was scared of sweet potatoes. It's okay, I now understand. 

6 months later, I'm greeted by you. The thought passes by — what could have been if I hadn't been anxious. I'm sorry. Maybe we'd have been silly and nerdy together, but it's in the past now. I'd say, "I'm sorry, you didn't deserve that, my anxiety. I hope you have found someone less anxious, now." You'd maybe say, "You’re young". And, I'd say, "I grew up a sick child."

---

So I grew up a sick child. 

We lived in Powai. I was admitted because of asthma, and we moved to Thane. They told me, that I couldn't handle the pollution in Powai, so we moved. I was then put on inhalers, two puffs in the morning and two at night. Every year, the inhalers got stronger. My best friend and I would compete on who has stronger inhalers. Until I was 10, my windpipes opened up more, I could survive with inhalers with much less strength. But, that's when my migraines began. It started off occasional. After badminton, lathered in sweat, I could feel the heat against my body, and the aching in my head. It became a weekly thing. I got a CT scan, nothing showed up. It's a migraine. A combination of paracetamol and ibuprofen almost every other day. Another CT scan, nothing shows up. This keeps going on, fainting spells. I'm convinced, I'll live a short life. I must live like there's no tomorrow. 

I'm on amitriptyline. 

I'm 17. I have a headache. It's not going. Stronger meds. It's not going. Stronger meds. I can't move my head. The meds are weighing it down. It's painful, I lift my head. The world is spinning, it's heavy. I'll keep my eyes closed. They were thinking of putting me in the hospital. ICU. I've been there before. I've been to the PICU for food poisoning. The muscle relaxants worked. 

So what, it's TMD — some Temporomandibular disorder. I grind my teeth at night, apparently, so I now wear a mouth guard and sleep, and I have benzos. 

The benzos make me sleep a lot. I don't understand what's happening. So they decide I'm not on benzos anymore. 

I still wear a mouth guard. They decide I'm not on amitriptyline anymore. 

I don't understand what's happening. I want to die. I've had enough suffering. Take it away from me. I see an orange. 

I leave. I'm leaving, India. I get sick often for the first year. I get COVID once. Second year, I can't move from my bed, the second time that I got COVID. I get cold easily. Walking is painful. I'm tired all the time. All the time. 

I get the flu. 

I eat plantains, and I could feel my throat close up, voice go hoarse, my face was swollen. I'm taken to the ER, they give me meds, I'm scared, I feel things, I had this feeling, I got to sleep thinking I'm not going to wake up. 

I eat things, I react. I'm convinced I have MCAS, I'm going to die. 

I feel my heartbeat, in my sleep, it's uneven. I can only eat bread now with cream cheese. I'm scared of everything else. I don't have any benzos on me. I wonder, if I had them now, would I get a paradoxical effect anyway. I want to die. I can't breathe. And, I try. I can't. 

I get an allergy test. It validates my fears. I stop eating most things on it. Why did my body react to so many. I got a blood test too. They were different. Turns out neither are reliable. But, I'm scared so I stop. 

Until, I can't. I can't cook like this. I can't live like this. I eat slowly, scared. Nothing happens. So I try, and try. I react to some, I don't to others, I know it's in my head, I have Allegra, when I'm scared. 

I move to New York. It's different. You sat across me, I kept checking the time and scared of finding a piece of corn in my Chipotle. I'm not brave, I'm scared. You saw something, I couldn't.

---

You told me once, you could see yourself by the beachside drinking orange juice, awaiting for death to come by. You offered me orange juice once. I said no. But, all I want to tell you now is that I eat oranges, and I have a jug of orange juice in my fridge.

Thank you.
